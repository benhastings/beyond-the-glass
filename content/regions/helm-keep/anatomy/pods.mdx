<Step id="why-pods" title="Why a pod, not a container">

<Analogy
  then="A service on a server was a main process, sometimes with a helper beside it, sharing the host's address and disk."
  now="A pod is that small group: one main container, sometimes helpers, sharing one address and its volumes."
/>

A <Term id="pod">pod</Term> is the smallest thing Kubernetes runs. Usually it holds one container. When
two containers must always run together (a program and a helper that ships its logs, say), they go in the
same pod, so they're scheduled together and can talk over `localhost`.

Pods are disposable. They're created, they run, and when they're replaced the new pod has a new name and
a new address. Nothing should depend on one particular pod.

</Step>

<Step id="lifecycle" title="A pod's life">

<Depth>
  <ELI5>
    A new pod waits for a machine. Then its program starts. If the program keeps running, the pod is
    Running. If it ends, the pod says whether it ended well or badly.
  </ELI5>
  <Operator>
    **Pending**: accepted, but not yet scheduled, or still pulling its image. **Running**: on a
    node, with at least one container started. **Succeeded** or **Failed**: every container has
    ended (for one-off jobs). STATUS in `kubectl get pods` often shows something more specific, such
    as ContainerCreating, CrashLoopBackOff or ImagePullBackOff: the reason a container is waiting.
  </Operator>
</Depth>

</Step>

<Step id="where" title="Where is it running?">

`kubectl get pods` lists pods; `-o wide` adds each pod's IP address and **node**. Find where the notifier
runs.

<Step id="describe" title="Describe a pod">

`kubectl describe pod NAME` prints everything about one pod: its node, its containers with their image,
**State** and **Last State**, its conditions, and at the bottom the **Events**: what the cluster did to it,
in order. Names match by prefix, so `kubectl describe pod ravenpost-notifier -n ravenpost` is enough.
