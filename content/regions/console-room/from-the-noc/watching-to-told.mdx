<Step id="the-wall" title="The wall of screens">

<Analogy
  then="You watched the wall: host graphs, ping checks, a red light for every disk over 90%."
  now="Rules watch the metrics for you, and page a person only when users are affected."
/>

The wall worked when there were dozens of servers you knew by name. A cluster runs hundreds of pods that
come and go every day. Nobody can watch all of that, and most of it doesn't need watching: a pod that
restarts once and recovers is the cluster doing its job.

So the work changes from watching everything to being told about the things that matter.

</Step>

<Step id="symptoms" title="Symptoms, not causes">

A **cause** alert fires on something that might hurt: CPU high, a disk filling, a pod restarting. A
**symptom** alert fires on something that does hurt: senders getting errors, mail taking too long to
send.

<Depth>
  <ELI5>
    A cause alert says "this might be a problem". A symptom alert says "people are having a problem
    right now". Wake people for the second kind. Look at the first kind in the morning.
  </ELI5>
  <Operator>
    Page on symptoms users feel: error rate, latency, availability, measured where users meet the
    service (the gate, for Ravenpost). Cause signals (saturation, restarts, node pressure) go on
    dashboards and in tickets, and are where you look **after** a symptom pages you. One symptom
    alert covers many causes, including ones you didn't predict.
  </Operator>
</Depth>

<Step id="slos" title="Service level objectives">

An <Term id="slo">SLO</Term> is a target for how good the service should be, in the user's terms: "99.5%
of send requests succeed, measured over 30 days". The 0.5% that's allowed to fail is the **error
budget**.

Alerts built on an SLO ask one question: are we using up the budget fast enough to miss the target? A
short burst of errors that the budget can absorb waits for the morning. A steady burn that would empty it
by Friday pages someone now.

<Depth>
  <ELI5>
    An SLO is a promise, like "almost every email gets sent". A few failures are allowed. The alert
    goes off when failures pile up fast enough to break the promise.
  </ELI5>
  <Operator>
    Burn-rate alerts compare the error ratio over a window with the ratio the SLO allows. A fast
    burn over a short window pages; a slow burn over a long window opens a ticket. You'll build
    these in Signalwood.
  </Operator>
</Depth>

</Step>

<Step id="pages" title="Pages that matter">

Every page should mean: users are affected, and someone needs to act. If an alert fires and the right
response is "ignore it", it shouldn't page.

That's a relief more than a rule. Fewer pages, each one real, each one with a <Term id="runbook">runbook</Term>
link that says where to look first.
