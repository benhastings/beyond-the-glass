<Step id="what-stays" title="What you bring with you">

You already know how to keep a service up at three in the morning. You know what "normal" looks like on
a Tuesday, you change things carefully, and you stay calm when the wall turns red. None of that is
out of date.

What's changed is where the work happens. Ravenpost, the mail service you'll look after in this course,
doesn't run on servers you know by name. It runs in a cluster that places, restarts and replaces its
programs for you. Your job moves from doing those things by hand to telling the cluster what you want,
and checking that it did.

<Callout kind="habit">
  Four habits run through the whole course: **check your context, look before you touch, change
  through Git, and correlate signals.** You'll see each of them again and again.
</Callout>

</Step>

<Step id="pets-and-cattle" title="Pets and cattle">

<Analogy
  then="mail-02 was a server with a name, a history and a runbook page of its own. When it misbehaved, you logged in and nursed it back."
  now="A node is one of many identical machines. When one goes wrong, the cluster moves its work elsewhere and the node is replaced."
/>

<Depth>
  <ELI5>
    A pet has a name and you care for it. Cattle are a herd. If one gets sick, the herd carries on.
    A cluster treats its machines like a herd, so no single machine matters much.
  </ELI5>
  <Operator>
    Clusters assume machines fail. Work isn't tied to one host, so a failed or drained
    <Term id="node">node</Term> is an event the cluster handles by rescheduling, not an outage you
    fix by hand. You still care about nodes in aggregate (capacity, pressure, drains), but rarely
    about one by name.
  </Operator>
</Depth>

<Step id="desired-state" title="Desired state">

On a server, you ran commands to make things so: start this, copy that, restart the other. If something
changed afterwards, it stayed changed until someone noticed.

In a cluster, you write down how things **should** be: "three copies of ravenpost-api, version 1.14.2".
That written-down version is the <Term id="desired-state">desired state</Term>. Programs called

<Term id="controller">controllers</Term> compare it with what's really running, over and over, and
fix any difference they find.

<Depth>
  <ELI5>
    You write a list of what you want. A helper reads the list again and again. If what it sees
    doesn't match the list, it fixes it. You don't have to ask twice.
  </ELI5>
  <Operator>
    A <Term id="deployment">Deployment</Term> records the desired state for a service. Its
    controller creates and removes pods until the actual state matches, and keeps doing so. This is
    why a pod you delete comes back, and why a hand-made change can be quietly undone.
  </Operator>
</Depth>

<Step id="teammate" title="Automation as a teammate">

The cluster now does some of what you used to do at night: restarting a program that died, replacing a
failed machine, keeping the right number of copies running. That's help, not a threat to your job.

It also means two new skills matter more than before. You read what the automation is doing (its events
and history), and you change the desired state rather than the running system. Fixing the running system
by hand is like correcting the screen instead of the source: the next refresh puts it back.
